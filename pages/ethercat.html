EtherCAT

EtherCAT is an Ethernet-based fieldbus for connecting a controller to motor drives and I/O. It is the layer immediately under a robot's control loop: the loop computes a command, and EtherCAT is how that command reaches the drive and how the drive's state comes back.

Processing on the fly

The thing that makes it fast is that slaves do not receive, store, and forward frames. A single frame is sent by the master and passes through every slave in the network; each slave reads the bytes addressed to it and writes its own reply into the frame as it passes through, in hardware, with a delay on the order of a microsecond. The frame comes back to the master having visited everyone.

So the cycle cost is roughly one frame transit, not N hops of switching latency. A network of thirty drives can be serviced in well under a millisecond.

The process image

The master doesn't address slaves one at a time during the cyclic exchange. An FMMU (fieldbus memory management unit) on each slave maps that slave's local registers into a shared logical address space, so from the master's side the whole network looks like one contiguous block of memory — the process image. Read a byte range, get every drive's status; write another range, command them all.

Two kinds of data travel over it:

  • PDOs (process data objects) — the cyclic payload. Actual position, velocity, torque, status word out; target position and control word in. This is what the control loop touches every cycle.
  • SDOs (service data objects) — acyclic configuration, sent through a mailbox. Gear ratios, limits, PDO mapping itself. Used at startup, not in the loop.

With CANopen over EtherCAT (CoE), the drive exposes a CiA 402 object dictionary: 0x6040 control word, 0x6041 status word, 0x6060 mode of operation, 0x607A target position. The modes that matter for a high-rate loop are the cyclic synchronous ones — CSP, CSV, CST for position, velocity and torque.

The state machine

Slaves move through four states, and bring-up problems are usually a slave refusing to advance:

INIT      no communication beyond basic registers
PRE-OP    mailbox works; SDO configuration happens here
SAFE-OP   process data inputs valid, outputs not yet applied
OP        outputs applied — the drive acts on commands

A slave stuck in PRE-OP or dropping back to SAFE-OP almost always means a mismatch between the PDO mapping the master configured and what the slave expects, or a missing mandatory SDO. Useful practice is to report which slave failed to advance rather than that the transition failed, because on a thirty-node network that difference is an hour of debugging.

The working counter

Every datagram carries a working counter. Each slave that successfully processes its portion increments it. The master knows what the total should be, so a mismatch is an immediate, cheap, per-cycle health check: a slave has dropped off, or a cable is marginal, or something is not in the state you think it is.

Checking the working counter every cycle, and treating a mismatch as a fault rather than logging it and continuing, is the single most useful piece of defensive code in an EtherCAT master.

Distributed clocks

Frames reach slaves at different times, which means that without help, thirty drives would act on their commands at thirty slightly different instants. The distributed clock mechanism fixes this. One slave's clock is the reference; the master measures propagation delay to every other slave, computes offsets, and then continuously compensates drift. Slaves then generate a local SYNC0 event, and they all act on that — simultaneously — rather than on frame arrival.

This is a different concern from loop jitter and worth measuring separately. Your thread can wake perfectly on time and the bus can still be badly synchronised, or the reverse.
